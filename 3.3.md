# 3.3 How Text Actually Gets Cut Up: From Letters to Words Through Merging

The idea of naively numbering every word doesn't work — so we need a way to cut up text that handles frequent words, rare words, and even words that never appeared in the training data equally well. The algorithm that solves this in practice is called **BPE — byte pair encoding**.

It doesn't work the way it might seem at first glance — it's not "count each word's frequency once and split the sentence accordingly." The whole thing happens gradually, from the bottom up, and it happens once, ahead of time, while the tokenizer is being trained (not on every new request to the model).

The process starts at the smallest possible level — individual letters. Every word in the training text is first represented as a sequence of separate characters. From there, the algorithm repeats the same step thousands of times in a row:

1. Count which pair of adjacent symbols appears most often across the whole corpus
2. Merge that pair into one new "symbol" — no longer a single letter, but a slightly bigger chunk
3. Repeat — now among the updated, slightly larger chunks

With every iteration, the tokenizer's vocabulary grows to include increasingly larger fragments. Frequent words — because they (or most of them) show up again and again — tend to "fuse" into a single token fairly quickly. Rare words simply don't get enough repetitions to earn a merge, and stay broken up into several smaller pieces — sometimes syllables, sometimes almost individual letters.

That's exactly why BPE handles an ordinary word like "table" just as well as a made-up neologism or a typo: if the whole token was never in the vocabulary, it can always be assembled from smaller, already-known pieces. The tokenizer never has to throw up its hands and say "I don't know this word" — at worst, it just breaks it up a little finer.
