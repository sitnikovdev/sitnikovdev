**5.1 The Problem: Sequential Memory and Its Limits**

Before transformers, understanding text was handled by architectures like RNNs and LSTMs. Their logic was simple and, on the surface, natural: read a sentence the way a human does — word by word, left to right. Each subsequent token was processed based on what the model had "remembered" from the previous steps.

The problem surfaced with long texts. Information about the first word of a sentence had to pass through dozens of intermediate steps before the model reached the last word — and at every step, some of that information was lost. It's a lot like a game of telephone: a phrase passed through ten people in a row rarely resembles the original by the end. The longer the context, the more the model lost its grip on what was said at the start.

There was another limitation — less obvious at first glance, but no less important — and it was computational. Since each token is processed only after the one before it, the steps can't run in parallel: processing token #5 simply can't begin until token #4 is finished. This isn't a matter of convenience; it's a hard architectural dependency built into the very idea of "reading in order."

These two limitations — losing long-range connections and the impossibility of parallelizing computation — weren't two separate problems. They were both consequences of the same underlying choice: processing text sequentially. To move forward, what had to be abandoned wasn't some implementation detail, but that idea itself.
